Discussion

We compared histograms using 4 mm, 7 mm, and 10 mm bin widths. The 4 mm bin width shows more detail and still reveals that the bill-length distribution is not unimodal, but it has more small fluctuations between neighbouring bins. The 10 mm bin width is too coarse and wide, making the distribution look overly simplified. We prefer the 7 mm bin width for the report because it gives a cleaner and more presentable overall shape while still showing that the distribution contains more than one cluster. This makes it easier for readers to understand the main pattern without being distracted by too much bin-level noise.

Histogram Interpretation

The bill-length distribution is not unimodal because the dataset combines multiple penguin species. Each species has a different typical bill length, so pooling them together creates several clusters instead of one single peak. This suggests that species is an important grouping variable and should not be ignored when interpreting bill length.

Jitter Versus Beeswarm

Advantage

One advantage of the beeswarm plot is that it shows the density of bill lengths more clearly than the jitter plot. In the beeswarm plot, the points are arranged systematically, so it is easier to see where many penguins are concentrated, especially for Adelie around the shorter bill lengths and Gentoo around the mid-40s to low-50s.

Disadvantage

One disadvantage is that the beeswarm plot can look more visually packed and less familiar to readers than a jitter plot. The jitter plot is simpler to understand, but its random spreading makes the pattern look less organized.

Observation

The violin-with-box plot shows that Adelie penguins have noticeably shorter bill lengths than Chinstrap and Gentoo penguins. The Adelie distribution is concentrated mostly around the high-30s to low-40s mm range, while Chinstrap and Gentoo are shifted further right, mostly around the mid-40s to low-50s mm range. The box plots show that the median bill length for Adelie is lower than the medians for Chinstrap and Gentoo. Why Combine Violin and Box Plots

The violin plot shows the full distribution shape, making it easier to see where bill lengths are more concentrated for each species. However, it does not clearly show summary statistics such as the median and interquartile range. The box plot provides these summary statistics, but it hides the detailed distribution shape. Combining the two gives both the density pattern and the key numerical summaries in one visualization.

Comparison of Bill-Length and Bill-Depth Distributions

Bill length and bill depth separate the penguin species in different ways. From the bill-length visualizations in Section 3.2(e–j), Adelie penguins are the easiest to separate because they generally have shorter bill lengths than Chinstrap and Gentoo penguins. However, Chinstrap and Gentoo overlap quite a lot in bill length, so bill length alone does not clearly separate those two species.

From the bill-depth histogram in Section 3.3(b), Gentoo penguins are the easiest to separate because they generally have shallower bill depths than Adelie and Chinstrap penguins. Adelie and Chinstrap have deeper bill depths and overlap more with each other. Therefore, the two measurements do not separate exactly the same species: bill length mainly separates Adelie from the others, while bill depth mainly separates Gentoo from the others.

Birate View: Bill Depth Versus Bill Length

Ungrouped Scatter Plot

ggplot(penguins_bill, aes(x = bill_len, y = bill_dep)) +
  geom_point(alpha = 0.6) +
  geom_smooth(method = "lm", se = TRUE) +
  labs(
    title = "Bill Depth versus Bill Length",
    x = "Bill length (mm)",
    y = "Bill depth (mm)",
    caption = "Data source: Gorman et al. (2014)"
  ) +
  theme_minimal()
`geom_smooth()` using formula = 'y ~ x'

Observation

The ungrouped scatter plot shows the relationship between bill length and bill depth for all penguins combined. Each point represents one penguin, and the blue regression line shows the pooled linear trend across all species. The line slopes downward, suggesting that when species is ignored, penguins with longer bill lengths appear to have lower bill depths. However, the points seem to form several clusters, so the pooled trend may be hiding species-level patterns.

Interpretation Of The Pooled Hrend

If species information were unavailable, a reader would infer a negative relationship between bill length and bill depth. In other words, the pooled trend suggests that penguins with longer bills tend to have shallower bills. The confidence band around the regression line is fairly narrow and does not suggest a flat relationship, so the pooled trend appears statistically meaningful. However, this interpretation may be misleading because the plot combines different species into one group.

Discussion

There are 4 points hidden by exact overlap. The maximum number of penguins stacked at a single (bill_len, bill_dep) coordinate is 2. This means exact duplicate coordinates exist, but the amount of exact overlap is quite small compared with the full cleaned dataset.

Exact overlap is not the only cause of overplotting. Even if two points do not have exactly the same bill length and bill depth, they can still be very close together visually, especially in dense regions of the scatter plot. This can make the plot look crowded and can make it difficult to judge where most observations are concentrated.

Assessment of Scatter Plot Variants

We would submit the jittered scatter plot to a journal referee. The exact-overlap check showed that only 4 points were hidden by identical coordinates, with a maximum stack size of 2, so exact duplicate points are not a serious issue in this dataset. The count-symbol plot shows exact overlaps, but the large circles make the plot look more crowded and harder to interpret. The jittered scatter plot makes dense regions easier to see while still preserving the overall negative pooled trend, so it is the clearer option for communicating the pattern.

Observation

Mapping species to colour reveals that the penguins form three separate species clusters. Adelie penguins generally have shorter bill lengths and deeper bills, Gentoo penguins have shallower bill depths, and Chinstrap penguins tend to have longer and deeper bills. The regression lines for all three species slope upward, showing a positive relationship between bill length and bill depth within each species. This pattern was hidden in the ungrouped scatter plot, where the pooled regression line sloped downward. The apparent reversal between the pooled trend and the species-specific trends is an example of Simpson’s paradox.

Verifier Task

overall_cor <- cor(penguins_bill$bill_len, penguins_bill$bill_dep)
round(overall_cor, 3)

penguins_bill |>

    species     r
1    Adelie 0.391
2    Gentoo 0.643
3 Chinstrap 0.654

The overall correlation is -0.235 — negative, matching the downward-sloping pooled line. Within each species every correlation is positive, matching the upward-sloping per-species lines in the grouped plot. This confirms Simpson’s Paradox in the numbers: the sign of the relationship literally flips depending on whether you account for species.
