In a professional report, the version with a bin width of 4 (or potentially 7, depending on how the data looks) would most likely be used. The version with a bin width of 10 would almost certainly be rejected.

Here is the breakdown of why bin width selection matters and how to choose the right one for your report:

1. The Verdict on the Three Options
Width = 4: The Strongest Candidate
Why: Bill lengths in the penguins dataset generally range from about 32 mm to 60 mm (a spread of roughly 28 mm). A bin width of 4 divides this range into about 7 distinct bins. This strikes a great balance: it provides enough granularity to show the shape of the data (such as whether it is bimodal or skewed) without making the plot look overly jagged.

Width = 7: The "Macro" Alternative
Why: This divides the data into only about 4 bins. While it simplifies the visual, it is on the edge of being too broad. It might be acceptable if your report's audience only needs a very high-level, simplified summary, but you risk smoothing out important details.

Width = 10: The Flawed Option
Why: A bin width of 10 is far too large for this dataset. It forces the entire data range into just 2 or 3 massive blocks. This completely hides the underlying distribution, masking features like data peaks, gaps, or the natural variations between penguin species.

2. The Core Principle: The "Goldilocks" Rule
Choosing a bin width for a report is about balancing oversmoothing and undersmoothing:

Too Wide (Oversmoothed): When bins are too wide (like width = 10), you aggregate too much data together. This creates a "blocky" graph that hides the true structure, shapes, and patterns of the data.

Too Narrow (Undersmoothed): If you went the other direction and chose a bin width of 0.1, the histogram would look like a jagged comb. You would end up plotting random noise and sampling variations rather than the actual trend.

Just Right: The ideal bin width reveals the overall "signal" (the true distribution pattern) while filtering out the "noise" (minor individual variations).

3. What to Look for When Choosing for a Report
When finalizing the graph for your report, look closely at the shape the bin width creates. In the case of the Palmer Penguins dataset, the bill length distribution is actually bimodal (it has two distinct peaks) because different species have drastically different average bill sizes.

A bin width of 4 preserves and highlights those two distinct peaks, which tells an interesting biological story.

A bin width of 10 lumps them all together into a single, uninformative wall of data.

Recommendation for your report: Generate all three, and select the finest width (width = 4) that still maintains a clean, continuous shape without gaps between every single bar.

To create the faceted histogram with a global mean anchor line and facet-specific jittered rugs, you can use the following R code.

To ensure the vertical line reflects the mean of all penguins combined—rather than calculating a separate mean for each facet—we calculate the global average beforehand and pass it as a static numeric value to xintercept.

R
library(ggplot2)
library(tidyverse)

set.seed(3107)

overall_mean_bill_len <- mean(penguins_bill$bill_len)

ggplot(penguins_bill, aes(x = bill_len)) +
  geom_histogram(
    binwidth = 7,
    boundary = 0,
    fill = "grey75",
    color = "white"
  ) +
  geom_rug(
    aes(y = 0),
    sides = "b",
    alpha = 0.18,
    position = position_jitter(width = 0.08, height = 0)
  ) +
  geom_vline(
    xintercept = overall_mean_bill_len,
    linetype = "dashed"
  ) +
  facet_wrap(~species) +
  labs(
    title = "Bill Length Distribution by Species",
    x = "Bill length (mm)",
    y = "Number of penguins",
    caption = "Data source: Gorman et al. (2014)"
  ) +
  theme_minimal()
```

Chinstrap and Gentoo penguins generally have longer bill lengths, with many observations around the mid-40s to low-50s. The dashed vertical line shows the overall mean bill length across all penguins and provides a common reference point across the three facets. This helps explain why the ungrouped histogram was not unimodal: it combined species with different bill-length distributions.
